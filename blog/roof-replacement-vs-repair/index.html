Roof Replacement vs Repair: How to Know Which One Your Home Actually Needs

Published August 22, 2026 · Stonnex Roofing

Your roof is leaking, the shingles look rough, or your insurance renewal just landed and now you're wondering how bad things actually are. The question most homeowners in the Lower Mainland hit first: do I need a full replacement, or can I get away with a repair?

Honest answer: it depends on age, damage type, and what's happening underneath. Here's how to work through those factors so you can make the call with confidence.

The core question: surface damage or structural failure?

Repairs make sense when the damage is isolated and the rest of the roof is sound. Replacement makes sense when the damage is widespread, the system is aging out, or the decking underneath is compromised.

That distinction sounds straightforward. In practice, it's hard to judge from the ground — because the most expensive problems on a BC roof are the ones you can't see without getting up there.

What a repair actually fixes

A repair addresses a specific failure point: a cracked or missing shingle, a failed flashing seal around a chimney or skylight, a small area of lifted material after a windstorm. Done correctly on a roof that still has life in it, a repair can buy you years.

What it doesn't fix is systemic deterioration. If shingles across your whole roof are granule-depleted, curling at the edges, or showing moss damage that has worked under the surface, patching one section doesn't change what's happening to the rest.

What replacement actually addresses

A full replacement removes everything down to the decking, lets a contractor inspect and replace any rotted or damaged sheathing, and installs a complete new system — fresh underlayment, flashing, and membrane layers included. You're starting from zero on the roof's lifespan.

On the coast, that matters. Our wet coastal climate accelerates deterioration in ways that don't always show up as obvious leaks until the damage is already significant.

Age is the most reliable indicator

Material lifespans vary, but age is still the clearest signal for whether repair or replacement is the right call.

MaterialTypical lifespan on the BC coast
Architectural asphalt shingle20–30 years
Cedar shake20–30 years (with maintenance)
Standing-seam metal40–70 years
Torch-on / flat membrane15–25 years
Slate50–100 years

If your roof is within 5 years of the end of its expected lifespan, a repair is often money spent on borrowed time. You'll pay for the repair now and the replacement in a few years anyway. In that scenario, replacement is usually the better financial decision.

If your roof is 10 or more years from end-of-life and the damage is genuinely isolated, a repair is the right call.

The 50% rule of thumb

A widely used guideline in the trade: if damage or deterioration covers more than 30–50% of the roof surface, replacement is almost always more cost-effective than repeated patching. Below that threshold, a targeted repair makes sense.

This isn't a hard rule, but it reflects a real pattern. Widespread granule loss, moss that has penetrated multiple shingle layers, or flashing failures across several penetrations aren't isolated problems — they're signs the whole system is aging out.

Conditions that push toward replacement

Decking damage

If the sheathing beneath the shingles is soft, delaminated, or rotted, no surface repair fixes that. This turns up regularly on homes in Burnaby, North Vancouver, and the Fraser Valley where older roofs have had slow leaks for years. The decking needs to come out, which means the roof comes off anyway.

Multiple leak points

One leak is a repair. Two or three leaks in different areas of the same roof are a pattern. At that point, the system is failing broadly, and chasing leaks one at a time gets expensive fast.

Failed or inadequate underlayment

The underlayment is the waterproofing layer beneath your shingles. If it's dried out, cracked, or was originally installed to a lower standard, new shingles on top won't protect you. This is common on homes built in the 1980s and 1990s across Langley, Surrey, and Coquitlam.

Insurance or sale requirements

If you're renewing home insurance or preparing to sell, an insurer or lender may require an inspection report. If that report flags the roof as near end-of-life or showing significant wear, a repair may not satisfy their requirements. Replacement gives you a clean report and a documented warranty.

Conditions where repair is the right answer

Storm damage on a younger roof

A windstorm lifts a section of shingles on a 10-year-old architectural asphalt roof. The rest of the roof is in good condition. That's a clear repair scenario — replacing a sound roof because of isolated storm damage is unnecessary.

Single flashing failure

Flashings around chimneys, skylights, and vents are common failure points, and they wear out independently of the shingles around them. A failed flashing seal is a repair, not a replacement trigger, as long as the surrounding material is sound.

Flat roof blistering in a limited area

On torch-on membrane systems — common on coach houses and secondary suites across Metro Vancouver — small blisters or seam separations in a defined area can be repaired without pulling the whole membrane. Age and overall condition still matter here.

What a proper inspection tells you

Guessing from the ground means you're only seeing the surface. A proper inspection covers:

  • Shingle condition across the full roof surface
  • Granule loss patterns (a sign of age and UV degradation)
  • Flashing condition at all penetrations and transitions
  • Gutter attachment and drainage performance
  • Decking condition at any visible soft spots or sag
  • Attic ventilation and signs of moisture from below

The cost question

Repair costs less upfront. Replacement costs more upfront but resets the clock on your roof's lifespan and typically comes with manufacturer warranties — on qualifying installations, those extend up to 50 years on materials from IKO, GAF, and Malarkey. That's a warranty on the product itself, not workmanship, but it's meaningful protection on a long-term asset.

The honest trade-off: if you repair a roof that needs replacing, you're delaying a cost, not avoiding it. If you replace a roof that only needed a repair, you've overspent. Getting the call right is worth the time it takes to get a proper inspection. A quick decision framework

Use this as a starting point before you call anyone.

Lean toward repair if:

  • Your roof is less than 15 years old and otherwise in good condition
  • The damage is isolated to one area or one failure point — a flashing, a few shingles, a small membrane section
  • No signs of decking damage or widespread moisture intrusion
  • A single storm event caused the damage

Lean toward replacement if:

  • Your roof is past 20–25 years on asphalt or cedar shake
  • Damage or wear covers more than a third of the surface
  • You have multiple leak points in different areas
  • An inspection has flagged decking damage or failed underlayment
  • You're preparing for a home sale or insurance renewal and need a clean report

Frequently asked questions

Can I repair part of my roof instead of replacing the whole thing?

Yes, if the damage is genuinely isolated and the rest of the roof is in good condition. A partial repair on a sound roof is a legitimate approach. The problem is when “partial” damage is actually a symptom of broader deterioration. A written inspection report tells you which situation you're in.

How do I know if my roof needs replacing or just repairing?

Age, damage scope, and decking condition are the three main factors. A roof past 20–25 years with widespread wear almost always needs replacing. A younger roof with isolated damage is usually a repair candidate. An inspection removes the guesswork.

Is it worth repairing a roof that's close to end of life?

Rarely. If your roof has 3–5 years of life left, a repair buys you a short window at a real cost. Most homeowners in that position are better off planning a replacement before a failure forces the issue on a bad timeline.

What does a roof inspection report include?

A thorough inspection covers shingle condition, granule loss, flashing integrity, decking condition, gutter attachment, and attic ventilation. At Stonnex, the written report is free with no obligation and is accepted by major BC insurers and lenders. Optional drone imagery and thermal assessment are available.

How much does roof repair cost compared to replacement in Metro Vancouver?

Pricing depends on the scope of damage, material type, and roof complexity. For replacement cost context, the Metro Vancouver roof replacement cost guide covers the main variables. The instant quote tool on the Stonnex site generates address-based pricing estimates in about 60 seconds.

Does a new roof come with a warranty?

Yes. On qualifying installations, manufacturer warranties on materials extend up to 50 years depending on the product and manufacturer. That covers the materials themselves. Workmanship warranty terms are separate — confirm those with your contractor at the time of quote.

What happens if I delay a roof replacement?

A failing roof that isn't replaced typically leads to water intrusion, which damages insulation, decking, and interior finishes. In the Lower Mainland's wet coastal climate, that damage accumulates faster than in drier regions. What starts as a deferred replacement often becomes a much more expensive project once decking repairs and interior work are factored in.
